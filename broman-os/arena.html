The problem: picking an agent harness is hard

Most comparisons of AI agents are written by someone selling one of them. I have a stake too: I run my own setup. So I put it in the ring with the others, under a codename, and I show the test where it could have lost.

Think of six kitchens. Some can buy from any supplier. Some must buy everything from one. The food may be just as good. But only the first kind can buy the cheap onions for the soup and save the expensive ones for the main course. That is provider lock-in, and it is the first thing that makes the choice hard: the feature list looks the same either way.

Lock-in and the token bill

The second thing is cost. Token cost was the most common complaint in the Hacker News threads, from about 70 people. The most common fix, from about 31, was to send routine work to cheaper models. You can only do that if the harness lets you choose the model.

Read this as one builder's evaluation, not a benchmark. Only one head-to-head test was run, with one or two runs per task. The rest comes from public docs, launch pages and Hacker News comments. Each cell in the table says which.

Three things from the threads shape the arena:

  • Less swarm, more gate. Many tried big many-agent setups and went back to one main session with a few helpers. What they said worked is boring: plan first, give each task its own copy, make a check pass before anything counts as done, have a second reviewer, keep a person on the merge.
  • People leave big frameworks. In the largest setup thread, 21 people said they had dropped a large framework such as OpenClaw, Hermes or Gas Town, mostly because updates broke things. Four still liked theirs. About 22 said: keep a personal fork instead.
  • The closed launches were judged on trust. For both Muse (at least 44 people) and Grok Bot (15), the top complaint was who made it, not what it did. One widely shared security claim about Muse was disputed in the threads, and I treat it as unproven.

The six contenders

  • Our setup. A self-hosted setup that runs everyday assistants and a small team of coding agents. It picks a model for each request.
  • Hermes Agent. An open-source agent from Nous Research, under the MIT licence. It runs on your own machine or server and talks to you through the terminal or chat apps. In our trial it refused any model with less than a 64,000-token context window.
  • OpenClaw. An open-source personal agent. Its docs list a long set of model providers, local models and fallback chains.
  • Claude Duet. Anthropic's proactive agent, announced on 21 October 2026 as a research preview. It is a separate app that works when you are away and checks with you before it acts. It is built on Claude Opus 5.5, with a waitlist for paid US plans.
  • Meta's Muse. A personal agent from Meta, launched in September 2026. Each user gets a cloud computer, and a Mac app can read local data if you allow it.
  • Grok Bot. An agent teammate from SpaceXAI, launched in August 2026. Each bot gets its own always-on computer, and bots can talk to each other.

The solution and the evidence

The short answer: the biggest split is not features. It is whether you can choose the model. Claude Duet, Meta's Muse and Grok Bot each run on their maker's own models. That rules out sending easy work to a cheaper model, which is the main way people in the Hacker News threads save tokens. Hermes and OpenClaw are open source and work with many providers. Of those two, Hermes is the one we tested head to head, and I see it as the main rival. In that test our setup passed 5 of 5 runs and Hermes passed 3 of 5, at about the same cost. The sample was tiny.

Open or locked: why lock-in decides the token bill

The cheap-model fix from the problem section only works where the harness lets you choose. Open harnesses do. Locked ones make the choice for you.

One correction to my own starting point. I went in thinking Hermes was the only open rival, because the others were locked to one provider. OpenClaw is not locked. Its docs list a dozen providers and local models. So the open camp has two outside contenders, not one.

Why I still name Hermes the main rival. It is the one we ran side by side with ours, and the one we keep borrowing ideas from. In the threads, many people said they had left large frameworks such as OpenClaw after updates kept breaking. That is a reason to watch OpenClaw less closely, not a test result.

Routing is not the whole bill. In our test, Hermes sent a smaller prompt for a plain chat: about 2,900 tokens to our 5,200. A lean prompt saves tokens on every call. Routing saves on the expensive calls. A good harness needs both.

Where Hermes won. A leaner chat prompt. An easier install: one command. Scheduled jobs that can run a script with no model at all, so they cost no tokens. A one-command backup. These are on my list to borrow.

Gaps and tech debt

What the evidence above does not cover, and the weak spots I know about.

  • One small test. One or two runs per task, on one free model. A lean, not a proof.
  • Two halves of our setup. The head-to-head ran its everyday-assistant side. The tier list below scores the setup as a whole, coding side included.
  • Four contenders were not run. Duet, Muse and Grok Bot are described from launch pages and comments. OpenClaw is described from its docs, and three of its cells have no source read.
  • Our own gaps. Old sessions are kept but not yet searchable. Against our own 30-point checklist, our setup scores 6 have, 18 partly, 2 lack and 4 not needed, and 14 of those marks still need a test. The biggest gaps are retrying failed calls with a growing delay, and packaging an agent so it installs on another machine. I have not scored the other five on all 30 yet.
  • Approval rules are prose. "Drafts, not sends" and "ask before destructive" are written as rules the model reads, not as hooks that block. Omnigent and Claude Code's own docs both point to enforced policy instead.
  • Isolation is worktrees only. They keep files apart but not the network or credentials.
  • Still to borrow from Hermes. A leaner chat prompt, scheduled jobs that need no model, and a one-command backup.
  • I built one of the six. I have tried to mark where each claim comes from so you can discount mine.
  • The comments are one community. Hacker News leans toward people who build their own tools.

What stands out: four problems others solve with less

Reading the grid part by part, four problems come up where another setup does the same job with less. For each: the problem, what others do, and where we stand.

  • Problem: instructions cost tokens before any work starts. Ordered stage folders (Jake Van Clief) give a stage only the files its inputs table names. Layers 0 to 2 are about 1.3 to 1.6k tokens, and one stage gets 2 to 8k, against 40k for one big file (the paper's figures, Van Clief and McDermott, arXiv 2603.16021). Claude Code caps each skill description at 1,536 characters and advises keeping its instruction file under 200 lines. Our base map is small, about 1.5k tokens.
  • Problem: approval rules written as prose can be ignored. Omnigent writes "ask before a shell command", "cap tool calls" and "hard spend cap" as short policies at three levels: server, agent and session. Claude Code's own docs call its instruction file "context, not enforced configuration" and point to a PreToolUse hook to block an action. We write the same ideas as prose rules ("drafts, not sends", "ask before destructive"), which the model may or may not follow.
  • Problem: worktrees isolate files, not the network or credentials. Omnigent wraps each agent in a sandbox, with nine providers plus bwrap and seatbelt. We rely on worktrees only.
  • Problem: stages need a contract. Ordered stage folders use a one-page contract per stage (inputs, process, outputs) that anyone can edit in a text editor. We reached the same design for our own factory stages.

Where ours carries more than the others:

  • Coverage. Our setup and the Kun Chen stack are the only two with no "lacks": all twelve parts are has or diff. Our setup has 10 outright has to Kun's 8. The reason is plain: it contains Kun's tools, so it inherits those rows. What it adds is the folder map and its routing, a goals layer, and a link layer that serves several tools. Next best are Troupe (7 has, a design only) and Claude Code (7 has, with no gate, no self-improvement and no goals).
  • One file serves five tools. The base rules file is linked into Claude Code, Codex, OpenCode, Gemini and backpass. OpenCode also reads Claude Code's file as a fallback, but no other setup treats the instruction file as a port that tools plug into.
  • Structural checks that cost no tokens. Checks and CI run on the map itself. I saw no test of the instruction files in any other setup. That is an absence in what I read, not proof.
  • A goals layer. Only Troupe and ours have one.

Harness-squared: a harness that edits itself

The problem. A rules file is only as good as the last time someone updated it. Our rule-fixing tool, backpass, runs offline over saved transcripts, edits one file, and a person applies each edit. It argues for an edit from past evidence; it never tests the edit.

What Google's project does. Harness-squared (Apache-2.0, last pushed 14 October 2026; paper arXiv 2610.14224, submitted 9 October 2026) works inside a task. It proposes, probes and composes edits to eight parts of the harness: system prompt, guardrails, memory, tool guidance, subagents, skills, scripts and plugins. After the task, a reflection step keeps the adopted harness and the lessons that have evidence, per domain, for next time. The reported gains are up to 11.5, 4.5 and 11.8 points on three benchmarks, on GLM-5 and Gemini 3.5 Flash. I found no independent replication, and the README and abstract describe no human step.

What I would borrow. Two ideas, both my own design suggestions; neither project does them for us.

  • Probe. Before an edit is accepted, run the candidates on the same task and compare the executions, with no ground-truth verifier. For backpass: replay one past task with and without a proposed rule, and show both outcomes on the review page.
  • A lessons library per domain. One lessons file per area of work, such as the blog or the factory, instead of one global map. Harness-squared's experiments notes describe the same per-domain idea.

Why not adopt the tool. It lists Linux, Python 3.12 and Vertex AI by default. Its Codex executor runs with the sandbox off and the network open. It is "not an officially supported Google product". And it has no human approval, which is the property our "drafts, not sends" and "ask before destructive" rules exist to keep.

Same family: Stanford Meta-Harness. It searches harness code using full execution traces; its examples use Claude Code as the proposer, and the authors include Omar Khattab and Chelsea Finn. The abstract reports +7.7 points on text classification and +4.7 on IMO math. If backpass is ever automated further, its filesystem-of-traces design is the closest match to our logs. Repo: stanford-iris-lab/meta-harness (MIT); paper: arXiv 2603.28052.

Folder as harness

A good kitchen is half the recipe. If the knives are where the knives go, you don't need a note saying where the knives go.

In a folder-controlled harness, the agent is steered mostly by where things are. There are still prompts, but they live inside files: a CONTEXT.md per stage, a numbered folder per step, a script next to the step that checks it. The folder is basically a GitHub repo.

Ordered stage folders (Jake Van Clief's Interpretable Context Methodology) work this way. Numbered stage folders carry the order; each stage has a one-page contract of inputs, process and outputs; one agent reads the right files at the right time. My context folders follow it.

Vercel's d0 is the other example. It's Vercel's internal agent that turns data questions into SQL. In "We removed 80% of our agent's tools" (Andrew Qu, 22 Dec 2025), the team says they replaced 17 specialised tools and heavy prompt engineering, and "stripped the agent down to a single tool: execute arbitrary bash commands". The agent (Claude Opus 4.5, per the post) reads their "Cube semantic layer as a directory of YAML, Markdown, and JSON files" with grep, cat, find and ls. On their five test queries, success went from 80% to 100%, runs got about 3.5 times faster, and token use fell 37%. They add that it "only worked because our semantic layer was already good documentation."

The d0 numbers are Vercel's own, from five queries. d0 is not scored on the twelve parts.

Why is a folder more deterministic than a prompt?

  • Versioned. Every change to a file is a Git commit. You can see when a rule changed and undo it.
  • Diffable. You can compare two versions line by line. A rule you typed into a chat is gone the moment the chat ends.
  • Reviewed. A change to the folder can go through review like code. No one reviews a prompt typed at 2 a.m.
  • Same steps every time. A script in the folder runs the same way on every call. The model still reads the files, but what it reads is fixed.

It's still not fully deterministic: the model chooses which files to read and what to do with them. That's why it sits between skills and scripts.

Glossary for the user-model part

For the "Goals and user model" part I borrow Honcho's words as a glossary only. Nothing from Honcho is installed.

  • Peer: anyone the agent talks with or about, a person or another agent.
  • Session: one run of back-and-forth between peers.
  • Message: one turn inside a session.
  • Conclusion: a fact the agent worked out about a peer from the messages.
  • Representation: the agent's running picture of a peer, built from its conclusions.
  • Peer card: a short summary of a peer's key facts, kept always at hand.
