What I'm trying to solve

  1. Most people and companies never write down what they actually want, so they can't tell progress from motion.
  2. I help them write it down. Then I build the software that closes the gap between where they are and that written goal.
  3. Every project is the same move: write the target down first, then let the tool work toward it.
  4. The causes I care about are effective altruism and 80,000 Hours, and AI safety.
  5. What I build for them is an intent-mapped AI-Life-OS, with harnesses and validators, so a crew of agents can be trusted with real work.
  6. For a person: “Your life, pointed at something you chose.” For a company: “Your company, pointed at one agreed goal.”

How we expect you to use AI

“While Parkinson's Law states that work expands to fill the time available for its completion, its inverse - Horstman's Law, the Law of Forced Efficiency - states that work contracts to fit the time allocated for its completion.”

This is how we expect you to use AI: give the work a short, fixed time box and let the AI crew help it contract to fit.

Parkinson's Law: C. Northcote Parkinson, The Economist, 1955. Horstman's Law: listed as “Horstman's Corollary to Parkinson's Law” among the Manager Tools podcast's principles (Mark Horstman).

Why we start from the ideal state

Write down where you are and where you want to be. Then write checks anyone could run to tell when you got there.

Credit

  • The method, from current state to ideal state through checks you can verify: Daniel Miessler, TheAlgorithm. The loop is set out in General Hill-Climbing, and writing what, not how, in Intent Engineering.

Notes and mental load

  • A specific plan for an unfinished goal stopped the nagging thoughts it caused, in lab studies (Masicampo and Baumeister, 2011). That suggests writing the plan down.
  • “There is an inverse relationship between things on your mind and those things getting done.” David Allen, as quoted on the Getting Things Done page. A method, not a study.
  • Working memory is limited, and design can reduce the load: cognitive load theory. Applying it to life planning is our reading.

Four habits, with sources

  • Time-box the work: Parkinson and Horstman, at the top of this page.
  • Protect long blocks for making things: Paul Graham, Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule.
  • Sort the urgent from the important: the Eisenhower matrix.
  • Spend effort where it multiplies: Andy Grove, High Output Management.
